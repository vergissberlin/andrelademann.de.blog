Continuing education at a company is never finished. Courses and certificates matter, but the real work starts when colleagues share where they got stuck, what they tried, and what they would do differently next time.

That is why we introduced guilds at Thinkport: a place to share knowledge across projects, compare experiences, and think about what comes next. At a client, admitting that an approach failed or that you do not know the answer can feel expensive. Internally, it should feel normal.

Two years in, though, the first excitement has faded. The people who cared deeply still turn up. Everyone else has a project deadline, a full calendar, and a perfectly reasonable reason to skip this one meeting. That is the hard part about guilds: the value is real, but the motivation does not maintain itself.

## A guild is where project work can become honest

Projects make us useful, but hard-won insight can stay trapped with the people who lived it.

A good guild creates a different kind of conversation. It is where someone can say, “I made the wrong technical call here”, without turning that sentence into a client escalation. It is where a colleague can show a rough prototype, a failed migration, or an uncomfortable lesson from a project and receive curiosity instead of judgement.

That is not a soft extra around delivery work. It is how a consultancy gets better at delivery work over time. If every hard-won insight remains private, every new project starts more or less from scratch.

## Goodwill burns first when the calendar gets crowded

The usual answer to falling participation is to ask people to care more. That rarely works. Most colleagues do care. They have to choose between a visible client commitment and an activity whose benefit might only show up later.

When guild work relies purely on enthusiasm, it quietly becomes unpaid emotional labour for a small group of reliable people. They prepare sessions, chase speakers, book rooms, write follow-ups, and make the meeting feel welcoming. Eventually, even the most committed organisers wonder why they are carrying it alone.

That is not a motivation problem in those people. It is a design problem in the organisation.

## Give the community an actual caretaker

The most practical intervention is a dedicated guild facilitator. Think of the role as a Scrum Master for the guild system: someone who does not own every topic, but makes it easier for the people with ideas to contribute.

That person can take the organisational weight off volunteers:

- keep a visible rhythm for each guild and help organisers plan a few sessions ahead;
- find internal speakers, pair them with a co-host, and make a first talk less intimidating;
- capture useful takeaways so a good session does not disappear when the video call ends;
- connect related guilds when a topic belongs to more than one community;
- remove friction around rooms, invitations, recordings, budgets, and internal channels.

Just as importantly, the facilitator can make the work visible. A short internal post about a useful session, a concrete lesson from a project, or a colleague who shared an honest failure tells the rest of the company that this work counts. Recognition does not need to be theatrical. It needs to be consistent.

## Make the contribution visible and useful

Visibility should not stop at applause. Guilds need a clear connection to the work people and the business actually care about.

For colleagues, that can mean a better network, a safer place to ask difficult questions, or a chance to build confidence by presenting an idea before bringing it to a client. For Thinkport, it can mean stronger project delivery, broader expertise, more reusable approaches, and credible stories about what the company can help clients with.

The link should be explicit. When a guild session leads to a reusable template, a better onboarding path, a small internal tool, or a new consulting angle, show that outcome. It proves that knowledge exchange is not a distraction from revenue. It is one of the ways a consultancy stays relevant enough to earn revenue in the first place.

There is a small trap here. Do not turn every guild into a sales pipeline or a performance metric. People need room to explore ideas that do not have an immediate billable outcome. The point is to make the value legible, not to squeeze every conversation until it becomes a quarterly target.

## Build a rhythm people want to return to

The format matters, but variety matters more. A guild that only runs slide presentations will eventually feel like another meeting. Let the format match the reason people are there.

One month might be a colleague explaining a decision that went badly. Another might be a short practical lab, a live review of a new tool, a roundtable with three people from different projects, or a five-minute lightning-talk session where nobody has to be an expert. Rotating the host and inviting newer colleagues to shape the agenda keeps ownership from settling with the same familiar names.

The useful ritual is also the simplest one: close the loop. Start each session with one thing that came from the last one. A conversation that turned into a template. An idea that helped on a project. A question someone finally found time to answer. When people can see the thread from meeting to meeting, they stop treating the guild as an isolated calendar event.

Technology is moving too quickly for a company to outsource all of its learning to external courses and vendor newsletters. We need spaces where people can compare notes while the change is still happening, not six months later when the trend has become a checkbox.

If we want colleagues to invest energy beyond the immediate project, we have to make that investment part of the work. Give guilds time, support, attention, and a person who keeps the system moving. Goodwill may light the first fire, but it will not keep it burning on its own.
