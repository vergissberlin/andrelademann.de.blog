I'm not an OKR expert. I won't pretend otherwise. What I *have* done is watch several companies try to introduce Objectives and Key Results — and trip over remarkably similar stones. Different industries, different sizes, same patterns. If you're about to roll OKRs out (or dust them off after a quiet failure), these patterns are worth recognising early. A second attempt costs money, trust, and attention you won't get back easily.

## Purpose and language get fuzzy fast

The first thing that slips is **why** you introduced OKRs at all. Alignment sounds good in the kick-off slide. Three months later, people treat the framework as another reporting ritual. Remind people — repeatedly — that OKRs exist to point the organisation in one direction and to use the potential of the people doing the work. Without that reminder, the process becomes theatre with nicer vocabulary.

Closely related: the abbreviation itself. "OKR" gets thrown around as if everyone shares a definition. They don't. **Objectives** are the direction you want. **Key Results** are how you know you're moving. When those blur, Key Results stop being measurable. "Improve customer satisfaction" is an Objective dressed up as a Key Result. "Raise NPS from 32 to 45 by end of quarter" is a Key Result.

**What helps:** name an OKR coach who people can ask without embarrassment. Use them — and, if you like, a careful AI review — to check drafts for clarity and measurability before a cycle starts. Cheap insurance against a quarter of vague ambition.

## Scope and sequencing: less, and only when the top is set

In the first iteration, teams almost always take on **too many** Key Results. Ambition feels productive. Mid-cycle, it feels like debt — and the framework, not the planning, takes the blame.

Keep the first cycle deliberately small: **at most three Objectives**, and **at most one Key Result per team member**. You can always add ambition later. You cannot easily recover from a cycle that "proved" the method doesn't work because everyone was overloaded.

Worse still: I've seen iterations start whilst leadership Objectives — the ones from the managing directors or the equivalent — were still undecided. That is a no-go. You cannot align teams to a direction that does not exist yet. Get the top layer written and agreed first. Everything else hangs off that.

## People and capacity are not a side topic

Some people will not warm to OKRs. That is not sabotage; it is human. A few feel unsafe when asked to own outcomes rather than tasks. Others prefer clear top-down instruction. Many are already overloaded and read OKRs as unpaid overtime with better branding.

Don't force enthusiasm. Lower the team's delivery velocity if you need room. Dedicated time for Key Results is not optional — it must be **bookable** like any other work, with an explicit rule for how those hours are recorded. Leadership has to protect that time. If the managing directors won't back people when the calendar fills with "urgent" firefighting, OKRs die quietly.

A named coach can run individual conversations with people who struggle. Link the cycle to personal development talks where that fits — personal goals and company Objectives don't have to live in separate universes. Light gamification can help some teams; it is optional, not a substitute for capacity and psychological safety.

## Steering without theatre

There are two failure modes for controlling, and both look bad from the outside.

Either Objectives and Key Results get forgotten until the last week of the cycle — or every OKR meeting turns into a status report where people defend percentages instead of solving problems. Too little steering and too much theatre produce the same outcome: nobody learns, and leadership loses confidence in the method.

Aim for a light cadence: short confidence check-ins focused on blockers and learning, not slide decks. At the end of the cycle, grade honestly, learn, and decide what to keep. Give one person ownership of the ritual so it doesn't evaporate when calendars get busy.

If you're introducing OKRs this year, which of these patterns already feels familiar in your organisation — and what will you change before the first cycle starts?
